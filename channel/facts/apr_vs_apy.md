# APR vs APY

- A credit card's interest rate is the price of borrowing money, and it is usually stated as a yearly rate called the annual percentage rate (APR). (source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-card-interest-rate-what-does-apr-mean-en-44/)
- On a mortgage, the APR adds points, broker fees and other charges on top of the interest rate, so the APR is usually higher than the interest rate. (source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-difference-between-a-mortgage-interest-rate-and-an-apr-en-135/)
- Annual percentage yield (APY) is the rate showing the total interest paid on a deposit account over a 365-day year, based on the interest rate and how often interest is compounded. (source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/2/)
- Federal rules example: a bank that pays $61.68 in interest over a year on $1,000 has an APY of 6.17 percent. (source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/a/)
- If a bank ad states a rate of return on savings, the rules say it must be stated as the APY; the plain interest rate can appear next to it but not more prominently. (source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/8/)
- Some card issuers turn the APR into a daily periodic rate by dividing it by 360 or 365, then add each day's interest to the balance, so interest compounds daily. (source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-daily-periodic-rate-on-a-credit-card-en-46/)
- Compounding example: $1,000 at 5 percent compounded once a year becomes $1,050 after 1 year and $1,102.50 after 2 years; compounding more often makes savings grow even faster. (source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-does-compound-interest-work-en-1683/)
